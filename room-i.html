Room I

Deposit

What is selected, purified and put away.

A plantation begins by choosing. One organism is isolated, judged against a standard, and separated from everything it grew among. A seed vault begins the same way. The three works in this room are all acts of putting away, and each one asks a different question about the moment of choosing: who decides, by what standard, and for whom.

  • Katie Paterson, Future Library, 2014–2114
  • Åsa Sonjasdotter, Cultivating Abundance, 2022
  • Amy Balkin, A People’s Archive of Sinking and Melting, 2012–ongoing
DS – I – 01 Room I · Deposit

Katie Paterson

Future Library, 2014–2114

Born 1981 in Glasgow; lives and works in Scotland.One thousand spruce trees, one hundred unread manuscripts, a purpose-built room.

In 2014 Paterson planted a thousand spruce saplings in Nordmarka forest outside Oslo. Every year since, one writer has handed over a manuscript that nobody is permitted to read. Margaret Atwood wrote the first.

The texts are held in a silent room lined with wood from the clearing, inside the Deichman library in Bjørvika, where visitors can see the sealed manuscripts but not open them. In 2114 the trees will be felled and the hundred texts printed on their paper. Paterson will be dead by then, as will every contributor and almost everyone who has ever stood in the room.

Why this roomThe purest deposit in the exhibition. Nothing about the work can be experienced now except the promise, and the promise depends entirely on an institution and a forest both surviving a century. It is the vault gesture stripped of everything except trust.

The label DS – I – 01

What is kept
One hundred manuscripts, unread, and the thousand trees that will print them.
Who deposited it
One writer each year since 2014, beginning with Margaret Atwood.
Who may withdraw it
Nobody, until 2114. The Deichman library in Oslo holds them in trust.Make a request →
What was left out
Everything those writers wrote on the other three hundred and sixty-four days.

The artist

Katie Paterson · Born 1981 in Glasgow; lives and works in Scotland.

Paterson works at scales that do not fit a human life. She has broadcast Beethoven to the moon and back, mapped every dead star, made a light bulb that simulates moonlight and buried a grain of sand carved to a two-hundredth of a millimetre back inside the Sahara. The method is consistent: take an enormous astronomical or geological fact, and find the one small object or gesture that lets a person stand next to it. Her collaborators are usually scientists, and the works are usually quiet. Future Library is the furthest she has pushed the approach, because the audience it is made for has not been born.

“Looking through the lens of deep time inevitably changes how you see the present.”

Katie Paterson, interviewed by Susan Mansfield, Scottish Art News, The Fleming Collection, 28 July 2026

Curators’ note

Stand in the Silent Room in Oslo and there is nothing to read. A hundred manuscripts, or the twelve that exist so far, sit behind glass in drawers with the author's name and the year on them, and nothing else. You cannot open them. Neither can the librarians. Paterson has made a collection whose entire value sits in a year none of us will see, and which depends on a public library and a forest both outlasting everyone now alive. That is the deposit in its purest form. It asks for trust and offers nothing back inside a human lifetime.

Afterwards, the artist’s own record of the work: futurelibrary.no

DS – I – 02 Room I · Deposit

Åsa Sonjasdotter

Cultivating Abundance, 2022

Born 1966 in Sweden; lives and works in Berlin.Film, 64 minutes, colour, Swedish with subtitles. Distributed by Filmform.

The film returns to the 1880s, when the Swedish Seed Association developed a breeding method built on ideas of genetic purity and originality. Varieties bred by farmers over generations were judged impure by that standard, pushed out of the fields and deposited in gene banks.

Sonjasdotter follows the plant breeder Hans Larsson and the seed alliance Allkorn as they bring those grains back into cultivation. She cuts gelatin-silver glass plates and moving images found in a cold store barn together with footage of the breeders at work. The purity standard, the film argues, was a fiction, and current seed law still rests on it.

Why this roomThis is the work that explains how a gene bank fills up. A standard of purity decides which plants are legitimate, the rest are archived, and a living practice becomes a stored sample. Sonjasdotter then films the reversal.

The label DS – I – 02

What is kept
Peasant-bred grain varieties, held in Nordic gene banks.
Who deposited it
Breeders who judged them impure by the standard set in the 1880s.
Who may withdraw it
Researchers, and since Allkorn, farmers who ask to grow them again.Make a request →
What was left out
The fields they came from, and the practice of breeding inside them.

The artist

Åsa Sonjasdotter · Born 1966 in Sweden; lives and works in Berlin.

Sonjasdotter is an artist and researcher who works through cultivation itself rather than through images of it. Her long project Potato Perspective began after she encountered the seed-sharing farmers of Navdanya in India, and took the cultivated potato as a way to trace how modernisation reorganised the relation between people and plants: Andean origins, EU variety registration, genetic engineering, and property rights over crops. She grows, propagates and exhibits the plants as the work. Cultivating Abundance continues that line into film, following breeders who are returning archived grain to the field.

Curators’ note

A gene bank does not fill up by accident. In the eighteen eighties the Swedish Seed Association settled on a method built around purity and originality, and grain that farmers had bred over generations failed that test. It left the fields and went into storage, alive but uncultivated, which is a strange way to survive. Sonjasdotter films the reversal: the breeder Hans Larsson and the seed alliance Allkorn asking for those grains back and putting them in the ground again. Her argument is that the purity standard was always a fiction, and that seed law still rests on it.

Afterwards, the artist’s own record of the work: filmform.com

DS – I – 03 Room I · Deposit

Amy Balkin

A People’s Archive of Sinking and Melting, 2012–ongoing

Born 1967; lives and works in San Francisco.Collection of contributed objects, vitrines, museum labels.

A growing collection of ordinary objects contributed by people living in places expected to disappear through climate change and the political and economic pressures that come with it. A tuna can, a sweet wrapper, a plastic toy bird, a bottle cap, a flag, a dollar bill soaked in a flooded office, water from Venice.

Contributions have arrived from Kivalina in Alaska, Dakar, Nepal, Venice, Cuba, Germany after the 2013 floods and New York after Sandy. Nothing weighs more than a kilogram. Each object is catalogued as though it were a historical artefact, and its label carries the contributing country’s status under the UN climate convention.

Why this roomA deposit with the selection step removed. Nothing is chosen: everything offered is accepted, and the whole of it is always shown together. Balkin does organise what arrives, using the contributing country’s status under the UN climate convention as the key, so the ordering happens after the fact rather than at the door. Set beside a seed vault, it asks what changes when nobody is deciding what deserves keeping.

The label DS – I – 03

What is kept
Ordinary objects from places expected to disappear. Nothing over a kilogram.
Who deposited it
Anyone. Individuals, consulates, passers-by in each host city.
Who may withdraw it
No one withdraws. The whole archive is always shown together.Make a request →
What was left out
Almost nothing. Two submissions were refused for misreading the brief.

The artist

Amy Balkin · Born 1967; lives and works in San Francisco.

Balkin makes work about the politics of land, air and atmosphere, and about who is permitted to own them. This Is the Public Domain is a parcel of California she has spent years trying to give to the public in perpetuity; Public Smog is a park in the atmosphere built from emissions credits she purchased and withheld from the market. The projects tend to run for years, to involve bureaucracies rather than studios, and to end in documents. A People's Archive of Sinking and Melting applies that patience to objects contributed by people living in places expected to disappear.

“The objects are artifacts of a decision to participate by contributing.”

Amy Balkin, in “What Will Have Been: Interviews on A People’s Archive of Sinking and Melting”, The Brooklyn Rail, June 2014

Curators’ note

Everything in this collection was handed over by someone who lives in a place expected to disappear. A tuna can. A plastic toy bird. A bottle cap. A dollar bill that was soaked when an office flooded. Nothing weighs more than a kilogram, and nothing is refused, which makes it the opposite of a seed vault: no expert decides what deserves keeping. Each object is catalogued like a historical artefact, and its label carries the contributing country's status under the United Nations climate convention, so the politics sits on the label rather than underneath it.

Afterwards, the artist’s own record of the work: archiveofsinkingandmelting.org
