Room II

Custody

Who holds the material, under what names and on what terms.

Once a thing is kept, somebody holds it. Custody is a question of names, of records and of permission. It is also where the plantation form shows itself most plainly, because a collection that controls access has to decide who counts as an owner. The three works here answer that in three incompatible ways.

  • Uriel Orlow, Theatrum Botanicum, 2015–2018
  • Vivien Sansour, Palestine Heirloom Seed Library, 2014–ongoing
  • Futurefarmers, Seed Journey, 2016–ongoing

DS – II – 01 Room II · Custody

Uriel Orlow

Theatrum Botanicum, 2015–2018

Born 1973 in Zurich; lives and works in London, Lisbon and Zurich.Films, sound works, photographs, slide projection and installation.

A body of work made between South Africa and Europe that treats the botanical world as a stage for politics. It covers plant migration and bio-piracy, flower diplomacy during apartheid, the garden that Mandela and his fellow prisoners planted on Robben Island, and the court case brought against the traditional healer Mafavuke Ngcobo.

The element shown here is What Plants Were Called Before They Had a Name, in which names carried by plants in South African languages before European botany renamed them are projected as light rather than printed as labels.

Why this roomCustody begins with naming. A plant that enters a collection under a Latin binomial enters as science; the same plant under its older name is filed as folklore, or not filed at all. Orlow shows the renaming as an act with a date and an author.

The label DS – II – 01

The artist

Uriel Orlow · Born 1973 in Zurich; lives and works in London, Lisbon and Zurich.

Orlow works in film, sound, photography and installation, usually across long research relationships with a particular place. Theatrum Botanicum came out of several years of work between South Africa and Europe and treats plants as political actors rather than scenery: the garden on Robben Island, flower diplomacy under apartheid, bio-piracy, and the court case brought against the healer Mafavuke Ngcobo. He is explicit that botanical collections are not neutral, and equally explicit about the terms on which he works in places that are not his own.

“Botanical gardens are often colonial archives, with plants from all over the world. There’s an epistemic violence in that.”

Uriel Orlow, quoted in “How Uriel Orlow Uses Plants to Tell Stories of Power”, Art Basel Stories

Curators’ note

Custody starts earlier than most people think. It starts at the moment of naming. A plant that enters a collection under a Latin binomial enters as science: it gets a herbarium sheet, a date, a collector, a place in a filing system that will still be searchable in two hundred years. The same plant under the name its neighbours used gets filed as folklore, or is not filed at all. Orlow projects those older names as light rather than printing them as labels, which is close to the only way they appear in a gallery at all.

DS – II – 02 Room II · Custody

Vivien Sansour

Palestine Heirloom Seed Library, 2014–ongoing

Born 1978 in Jerusalem; works in Battir, Palestine.Seed library, jars, ash, field work, travelling kitchen.

Sansour founded the library to find threatened Palestinian crop varieties and return them to the field and to the table. Around forty-seven heirloom varieties are kept in jars, coated in ash as farmers have long preserved them, on the upper floor of a traditional courtyard house in Battir.

Among them are jazar ahmar, a purple carrot cooked in tamarind, baladi tomatoes, Jadu’i watermelons and yakteen gourds. The library is an open room where farmers and neighbours come and go. Sansour keeps no record of who takes seeds or where they are planted.

“I don’t track the seeds; they become a part of people’s lives. I wanted the seeds to stay free. They don’t belong to me, they belong to themselves.”

Vivien Sansour, quoted in Shannon Lee, “Vivien Sansour Plants Seeds of Hope for Palestine”, Asian American Arts Alliance, 18 December 2023
Why this roomThe counter-vault, and the work the whole exhibition turns on. It performs every function of a seed bank and refuses the one that defines it: the register of who may withdraw. Set against Svalbard, it makes custody visible as a choice rather than a necessity.

The label DS – II – 02

The artist

Vivien Sansour · Born 1978 in Jerusalem; works in Battir, Palestine.

Sansour is an agricultural researcher and artist who founded the Palestine Heirloom Seed Library in 2014 to find threatened Palestinian crop varieties and return them to the field and to the table. The work is not primarily exhibition work: it is seed-finding, growing, cooking and conversation, carried by a travelling kitchen and a Traveling Seed Library as much as by any gallery. The holdings are kept in jars coated in ash on the upper floor of a courtyard house in Battir, in an open room, with no record of who takes what.

Curators’ note

This is the work the exhibition turns on. On the upper floor of a courtyard house in Battir there are jars of seed coated in ash, about forty-seven varieties: a purple carrot cooked in tamarind, baladi tomatoes, Jadu'i watermelons. It accepts material, keeps it alive and gives it back on request, which is everything a seed bank does. It refuses one thing. Nobody writes down who took what. Sansour has said she wanted the seeds to stay free, that they do not belong to her, they belong to themselves. Set against Svalbard, that single refusal makes custody look like a choice rather than a necessity.

DS – II – 03 Room II · Custody

Futurefarmers

Seed Journey, 2016–ongoing

Collective founded in 1995 by Amy Franceschini; based in San Francisco and Ghent.Sea voyage, cargo of grain seed, logbook, public gatherings.

In September 2016 a wooden rescue sailboat, the RS‑10 Christiania, built in 1895 to a Colin Archer design, left the port of Oslo carrying seed grain. It sailed to Vejle, London, Cardiff and Antwerp, then on through Jersey, San Sebastián and Santander, with Mallorca, Bethlehem and Istanbul ahead.

The crew rotates between artists, anthropologists, biologists, bakers, farmers and sailors, who unload and discuss the cargo at harbours, barns, museums and social centres along the way. The route runs backwards along the paths the grains originally travelled.

“Seed Journey moves people, ideas and seeds through time and space. The crew’s interests will influence the route, but ultimately grains are the compass.”

Futurefarmers, project text, futurefarmers.com
Why this roomCustody as hospitality instead of control. The seeds are neither frozen nor owned; they move, they are handed over at each port, and the itinerary is allowed to change. The work treats a seed as something you pass on.

The artist

Futurefarmers · Collective founded in 1995 by Amy Franceschini; based in San Francisco and Ghent.

Futurefarmers is a collective founded by Amy Franceschini in 1995, working between San Francisco and Ghent with farmers, scientists, architects and engineers. The group builds situations rather than objects: a windmill, a bakery, a boat, a shared meal, each one a device for getting people into a room around a question about food and land. Seed Journey is the clearest case, a sea voyage in a wooden rescue boat whose cargo of grain is unloaded and discussed at every port rather than delivered anywhere.

Held by the project film, sound and archives, at the source

  • The full voyage logbook, port by port

Curators’ note

In September two thousand and sixteen a wooden rescue boat built in eighteen ninety-five left Oslo with grain seed in the hold, and sailed to Vejle, London, Cardiff and Antwerp. At every port the cargo came out, and people gathered around it, and the grain was discussed and handed on. It is a storage strategy that works by the exact opposite of storage. Nothing is held at a fixed temperature and nothing stays still. The seed survives because it keeps moving and keeps being given to somebody else.
