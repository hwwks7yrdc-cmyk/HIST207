Curatorial statement

Inside a mountain on Spitsbergen, in a chamber held below freezing, sit boxes of seeds sent by gene banks around the world. Each is a duplicate, a second copy kept elsewhere in case the first is lost. Duplicate Sample gathers nine contemporary artists who work with this practice of keeping living things against a future nobody can see: seed libraries, gene banks, botanical collections, archives of material that is still alive.

Every such collection is a bet, and every bet is a selection. Somebody decided which varieties were worth the cold and which were left in the field, whose agriculture counted as heritage and whose counted as subsistence, who may withdraw a sample and on what terms. The exhibition holds two readings of the same rooms without settling between them. These places are genuine safeguards, built by people who expect to be useful. They are also monuments to an expected loss, and arrangements of power over who may hold what.

Anna Tsing describes two ways of living with other species. The plantation isolates one organism, purifies it, severs its relationships and controls access to it. The unruly edge is the margin where species collaborate without permission, and where, as she puts it, zones of neglect keep producing more life than any managed ground does. A seed vault is a plantation answer to loss, and a careful one. The works here are arranged along that axis.

The first room is about selecting and putting away. The second is about who holds the material, under what names and on what terms. The third is about what returns, what escapes and what refuses to be kept. Every work carries the same four-line label: what is kept, who deposited it, who may withdraw it, and what was left out. The last two are revealed on a click, so that looking behind the result is something you do.

The idea the exhibition runs on

The plantation, and the unruly edge

Anna Tsing begins from a single claim, and the whole essay follows from it.

“Human nature is an interspecies relationship.”

Tsing 2012, p. 141

From there she describes two opposed ways of living with other species. The first is the plantation: it isolates one organism, standardises it, strips away its relationships, and organises who has access. She points out that this is not natural and had to be learned.

“Because plantations have shaped how contemporary agribusiness is organised, we tend to think of such arrangements as the only way to grow crops. But this arrangement had to be naturalised until we learned to take the alienation of people from their crops for granted.”

Tsing 2012, p. 148

Against it she sets the unruly edge: the margin around managed space, where species collaborate without anyone’s permission. She makes a strong empirical claim about it.

“Evolutionary process in zones of neglect continues to produce more useful species and species interactions by many orders of magnitude.”

Tsing 2012, p. 151

Read through Tsing, a seed vault is a plantation answer to loss, and a careful one. It isolates a sample, holds it at a fixed temperature, separates it from the soil and the people it came from, and controls who may take it out. That is a description of a method, not an accusation: the method works, and it has saved material that would otherwise be gone.

It is also one method among several. This exhibition sets it beside the others.

How to read the exhibition

Every work carries the same label

Four lines, identical on all nine pages. What is kept. Who deposited it. Who may withdraw it. What was left out.

The first two are open. The last two are hidden until you click, so that retrieving the political content of a collection is a small act you perform rather than a sentence you are handed. By the third or fourth work you will be reading the fields before the picture, which is the point.

A thin rule down the right edge shows how deep you have gone. There is no menu inside a room. The only way through is down.

The label DS – II – 02

What is kept
About forty-seven heirloom varieties, in jars, coated in ash.
Who deposited it
Farmers across the West Bank, and the artist.
Who may withdraw it
Anyone who comes to the house in Battir. No record is kept.
What was left out
No accession list exists, so nothing can be audited, including loss.

Try it. This is the live label from Room II.
