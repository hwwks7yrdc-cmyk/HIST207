Room III

Thaw

What returns, what escapes, what refuses to be kept.

Tsing calls them unruly edges: the margins around managed space where species collaborate without anyone's permission. She argues that these zones of neglect keep producing more life than the managed ground does. This room is the edge. Material that nobody preserved turns out to have survived, and material that was managed gets away.

  • Maria Thereza Alves, Seeds of Change: A Floating Ballast Seed Garden, Bristol, 2012; series begun 1999
  • Precious Okoyomon, Earthseed, MMK Frankfurt, 2020
  • Kapwani Kiwanga, Flowers for Africa, 2013–ongoing
DS – III – 01 Room III · Thaw

Maria Thereza Alves

Seeds of Change: A Floating Ballast Seed Garden, Bristol, 2012; series begun 1999

Born 1961 in São Paulo; lives and works in Berlin and Naples.Barge, ballast soil, germinated plants. Made with the designer Gitta Gschwendtner.

Sailing ships carried soil, sand and rubble as ballast, taking it on in one port and tipping it out in the next. Alves locates that soil in harbour cities, excavates it, and germinates whatever is still dormant inside. Seeds that have waited two or three hundred years come up.

For Bristol she worked with the designer Gitta Gschwendtner to build a garden on a barge moored in the harbour, planted entirely from ballast seed, so that the flora growing there is a record of the city’s shipping routes and of the trade that ran along them.

Why this roomThaw in its most literal form. Nobody deposited these seeds and nobody kept them. They survived in the dark by accident and returned as evidence, which is what Tsing means when she says that zones of neglect keep producing what no collection could.

The artist

Maria Thereza Alves · Born 1961 in São Paulo; lives and works in Berlin and Naples.

Alves is a Brazilian artist long based in Berlin whose work recovers histories that official records leave out, often by going to the ground and digging. Seeds of Change, begun in the late 1990s and continued across European port cities, works from ballast flora: the seeds that travelled in the soil ships carried for stability and tipped out at the other end. She germinates that soil and reads what comes up as a record of the trade routes that produced it. She was a founder of Brazil's Green Party and has worked on indigenous land rights alongside the art.

“Celebrating the micro histories of plants and humans together.”

Maria Thereza Alves on Seeds of Change, University of Bristol, December 2016

Curators’ note

Ships once took on soil as ballast to steady themselves, and tipped it out at the other end. Inside that soil were seeds, and in the mud of Bristol harbour some of them waited roughly two centuries without anybody intending to keep them. Alves excavated that mud and let it germinate on a floating garden. What grows is a record of where the ships came from, which is a record of the trade that sent them. It is the only collection here that nobody built, and it is still a perfectly good archive.

DS – III – 02 Room III · Thaw

Precious Okoyomon

Earthseed, MMK Frankfurt, 2020

Born 1993 in London; raised in Ohio and Nigeria; lives and works in New York.Living kudzu, soil, sugar cane, water, lamb’s wool sculptures.

Okoyomon filled a museum with kudzu, a vine brought from Japan to the United States and planted across the South to hold eroding soil in place. It did not stay where it was planted. It now covers abandoned land across the region and is treated as a pest.

In the gallery the vine was allowed to grow over walls and floor for the run of the exhibition, through soil and sugar cane, around figures made of lamb’s wool. The plant was the artwork and also the thing the institution had to keep alive and could not fully control.

Why this roomA species introduced to solve a problem, which then escaped the terms of its introduction. Okoyomon reads that history alongside the forced movement of people, and lets the plant take the building. It is the clearest case of the feral in the exhibition.

The artist

Precious Okoyomon · Born 1993 in London; raised in Ohio and Nigeria; lives and works in New York.

Okoyomon is an artist and poet working between New York and Frankfurt whose installations are living systems: soil, water, plants, insects and sculpture left to carry on without the artist. They work with materials that have their own histories of forced movement, and let those materials behave. In Earthseed the material is kudzu, the vine introduced to Mississippi in 1876 and used to hold soil eroded by cotton cultivation, which then spread far past what anyone intended. The title comes from the religion invented in Octavia Butler's Parable of the Sower.

“The kudzu is so far from home.”

Precious Okoyomon, in conversation with Hannah Black, published in MMK’s booklet for Earthseed, Frankfurt, 2020

Curators’ note

Kudzu arrived in the United States as an ornamental and was then planted at scale to hold eroding soil in place, which it did, and then it kept going. In Okoyomon's installation it is allowed to grow over the room for the length of the exhibition, and the institution agrees in advance not to cut it back. Every other collection in this show has somebody deciding what comes out and when. This one withdraws itself, over whatever is nearest, on a schedule nobody controls.

Afterwards, the artist’s own record of the work: mmk.art

DS – III – 03 Room III · Thaw

Kapwani Kiwanga

Flowers for Africa, 2013–ongoing

Born 1978 in Hamilton, Canada; lives and works in Paris.Fresh floral arrangements made to a written protocol. Sixteen to date.

Kiwanga searches archives for photographs of the ceremonies at which African countries became independent, and looks at the flowers. She identifies each arrangement, takes the image to a florist in the exhibiting city, and has it recreated as closely as the record allows: a buttonhole, a table bouquet, an archway.

The protocol then states that the flowers must not be maintained. They wilt and collapse over the run of the show, and what a visitor sees depends entirely on when they arrive.

Why this roomAn archive that cannot be kept. Kiwanga reconstructs a document and then lets it decay on schedule, which is the exact opposite of the cold chain. It closes the exhibition by asking what preservation was ever promising.

The artist

Kapwani Kiwanga · Born 1978 in Hamilton, Canada; lives and works in Paris.

Kiwanga trained in anthropology and comparative religion before turning to art, and the method shows: her work starts in archives and ends in materials chosen for what they carry historically. Flowers for Africa, Linear Paintings and her work on the architecture of control all approach power through objects that seem too ordinary to be political. She has described the question of power asymmetry as the idea that pushes her most, and she is interested in what the non-human world remembers of events that human documents record badly or not at all.

“I think the natural world is a different kind of witness to human history.”

Kapwani Kiwanga, interviewed by Artnet News, 2021

Curators’ note

Kiwanga works from photographs of the ceremonies at which African countries became independent, and in those photographs there are flowers. She identifies the arrangement, takes the image to a florist in whichever city is hosting the show, and has it rebuilt as closely as the record allows. Then the protocol says: do not maintain it. It wilts and collapses over the run of the exhibition, so what you see depends entirely on when you came. It is the exact opposite of a cold chain, and it closes the show by asking what preservation was ever promising.
